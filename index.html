2. Recognize fake websites

Phishing links lead to pages that copy a real login screen. Read the domain, the part right before the first single slash, from right to left.

LinkVerdictWhy
https://accounts.google.com/signinRealThe domain is google.com; accounts is a subdomain.
https://paypa1.com/loginFakeThe letter "l" is replaced by the number "1".
https://google.com.secure-login.netFakeThe real domain is secure-login.net. Everything before it is just a subdomain.
http://amazon-support.xyz/verifyFakeBrand name plus an unrelated domain, an unusual ending, and no HTTPS.
https://bank.com@evil.siteFakeEverything before the @ is ignored. The browser goes to evil.site.

Padlock is not proof

HTTPS only means the connection is encrypted. Phishing sites use it too.

Hover before you click

The real destination shows in the status bar. On mobile, press and hold the link.

Type it yourself

Open the site from a bookmark or type the address instead of using the email link.

3. Social engineering tactics

Phishing works because it targets people, not software. Attackers rely on a handful of psychological triggers.

Urgency

"Act within 24 hours." Pressure stops you from thinking.

Authority

Messages that seem to come from your boss, IT, a bank, or the government.

Fear

"Your account was hacked" or "You will be fined."

Greed

Prizes, refunds, gift cards, or too-good-to-be-true offers.

Curiosity

"See who viewed your profile" or "Photos from last night."

Familiarity

A hijacked contact's account sending you a normal-looking file or link.

Other forms: spear phishing (targeted at one person), smishing (SMS), vishing (phone calls), and business email compromise (fake executive or vendor requests).

4. Real-world examples

Fake invoices, 2013 to 2015

A Lithuanian man posed as a hardware vendor and invoiced Google and Facebook. They paid over $100 million before the fraud was caught.

Clinton campaign, 2016

Campaign chairman John Podesta received a fake "Google security alert" email. He entered his password on a fake page, and thousands of emails were leaked.

Google Docs worm, 2017

Emails said a contact shared a Google Doc. The link asked to "allow access" for a fake app, and the message then spread to the victim's own contacts.

Twitter hack, 2020

Attackers phoned employees pretending to be IT support. They gained internal tool access and took over high-profile accounts to run a bitcoin scam.

5. Best practices

  • Stop and think. If a message creates panic, verify before acting.
  • Check the sender's full address, not just the display name.
  • Never click links or open attachments you did not expect.
  • Contact the company through its official app, site, or phone number.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA) on every account that offers it.
  • Use a password manager. It will not autofill on a fake domain.
  • Keep your browser, OS, and antivirus up to date.
  • Never share passwords, OTP codes, or card details by email, SMS, or phone.
  • Report suspicious messages to your IT or security team, then delete them.
  • If you already clicked or entered details: change the password, enable MFA, and tell IT immediately.

6. Types of phishing attacks

The goal is always the same, but the delivery channel and level of targeting vary.

TypeChannelHow it works
Email phishingEmailMass messages impersonating banks, delivery firms, or software vendors.
Spear phishingEmailPersonalized using details about you: your role, colleagues, or recent activity.
WhalingEmailSpear phishing aimed at executives and finance leaders.
Business email compromiseEmailA fake or hijacked executive or vendor account requests payments or changed bank details.
Clone phishingEmailA genuine earlier email is copied, with the link or attachment swapped for a malicious one.
SmishingSMS / chatTexts about parcels, KYC updates, or bank alerts containing a short link.
VishingPhone callCallers pose as the bank, tech support, or police and push you to share OTPs or install software.
QuishingQR codeA QR code on a poster, sticker, or email leads to a fake login or payment page.
PharmingWebVictims are sent to a lookalike site, sometimes by tampering with DNS, to capture credentials.

7. What to do if you are phished

Speed matters. Most damage happens in the first hours, so do not wait or feel embarrassed.

  1. Stop and disconnectClose the page. If you opened an attachment, disconnect the device from the network.
  2. Change your passwordUse a device you trust. Change it everywhere you reused it, and turn on MFA.
  3. Report itTell your IT or security team immediately. Follow their instructions for sharing the original email.
  4. Contact your bank if money or card details were involvedBlock the card and review transactions. In India, report financial fraud at cybercrime.gov.in or call 1930.
  5. Scan and monitorRun an antivirus scan. Check account login history and email forwarding rules, and watch statements for weeks.
  6. Learn from itNote which red flag was missed. Sharing the story helps colleagues avoid the same trap.

8. Key terms

Spoofing
Faking a sender name, address, or website so it looks legitimate.
Credential harvesting
A fake login page that captures usernames and passwords.
Typosquatting
Registering domains that differ by a small typo, such as paypa1.com.
Pretexting
Inventing a believable story to obtain information or access.
Payload
The malicious file or link that a phishing message delivers.
MFA
Multi-factor authentication: a second proof of identity beyond your password.
Passkey
A phishing-resistant login that only works on the genuine website.
OTP
One-time password sent by SMS or app. No genuine bank or company asks you to read it out.
